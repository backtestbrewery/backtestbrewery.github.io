More Than A Hack

2026-11-19 Football · Tracking Data · Machine Learning · Out of Sample 24 min read

The competition

More than a Hack 2027 is a football data hackathon run by Metrica Sports, FC Barcelona's Barça Innovation Hub and Mobile World Capital Barcelona. This year it asked one question and answered it with one number.

Here is a full professional match, FC Barcelona against Athletic Club. For every one of its 199,319 frames, who has the ball?

No jury, no pitch deck, no presentation. A script scores every team against a hidden answer key, and the score is the ranking.

199,319frames to label
25frames a second
41%of graded frames with no ball
17possessions of public answers
46tracking slots for 22 players
0hand labels allowed
4weeks from data to deadline
1number decides: spellF1

The match is LaLiga 2024-25, matchday 2, recorded by two cameras: a wide tactical camera that sees the whole pitch, and the TV broadcast with its cuts and replays. Every team got the same bundle: both videos, automatic tracking of the players and the ball, automatic tactical tags, and the maps from each camera to the pitch. All of it machine-made, none of it corrected.

The rules were short. Any method, any external data, LLMs welcome. Three things banned: labelling the match by hand, touching the hidden answer key, and submitting a file your public code can't regenerate exactly. Teams of three to five. Data out on 20 October, deadline 17 November, results before 26 November.

I entered with my team, EM FC. I'm not a football analyst. I build trading strategies and then try to break them, which is what this blog is mostly about. When the data arrived it looked a lot like a tick feed: a noisy, gappy stream of numbers, 25 updates a second, where the thing you actually care about is hidden and has to be inferred. So I treated it like one.

This post is the full report on our final submission, written the way I'd write up a backtest. The headline numbers first, then the data, the signal, the costs, the out-of-sample tests, what failed, and what I'd do next. Along the way I'll point out where each idea came from, because almost every one of them came from markets. It's written before the results are out, which feels like the honest way to write it.

§ 2The Problem, in Plain Football

Every frame gets one of four labels:

  • H: Barcelona (home) have the ball and it's in play.
  • A: Athletic (away) have it.
  • D: dead ball. Out of play, a foul, a goal, a substitution. Nobody "owns" it.
  • X: no answer. Free outside the two halves, and never a good idea inside them.

Possession changes only on control. A touch, a deflection, a header nobody collects: none of these hand the ball over. A loose ball stays with the last team that controlled it. That one word, control, turns out to shape the whole solution.

What makes it hard is the data:

  • The ball is missing on 41% of graded frames. Not flicker: long blackouts, mostly while play is stopped.
  • 46 tracking slots for 22 players. Tracks appear, vanish and swap identity as the tracker loses and finds people. There are no player names.
  • The goalkeeper slots carry the wrong team at times. One of them stands in the other team's box for thousands of frames. My fix: each keeper slot takes, frame by frame, the team that defends the goal in its half.
  • Units lie. Player speeds are labelled km/h and are actually m/s.
  • Only 5 minutes of answers are public. The other 101 minutes of graded play stay hidden until the results.

If you've ever cleaned exchange data, this list feels familiar: missing ticks, symbols mapped to the wrong instrument, wrong units. The first job is always the same. Make the data say what it claims to say.

§ 3Read the Scoring Rule First

The first thing I do with any strategy is read exactly how it will be judged. Sharpe, drawdown, hit rate: each one rewards a different strategy, and optimising the wrong one builds the wrong thing. This competition reports two numbers and ranks on one of them.

macroF1 is the per-frame view: how often each frame has the right label, averaged over H, A and D. spellF1 is the one that ranks. It cuts both the truth and your answer into possessions, a possession being an unbroken run of H or of A, then pairs each predicted possession with a true one of the same team if they overlap enough:

\text{IoU}(p, g) = \frac{|\,p \cap g\,|}{|\,p \cup g\,|} \qquad\qquad \text{spellF1} = \frac{1}{5}\sum_{\tau \in \{0.3,\,0.4,\,0.5,\,0.6,\,0.7\}} \text{F1}_{\tau}
(1)

A pair counts as a hit when its overlap (IoU) clears the bar \tau. Hits, misses and false alarms give an F1 at each bar, and the five are averaged.

The organisers prove why this matters with their own baseline: the nearest player to the ball, then a majority vote over a moving window. Same algorithm four times, only the window changes.

Figure 1. The organisers' baseline on the full match with four window lengths. The per-frame score barely moves. The possession score moves five-fold. The 2 s version is the published floor.

The per-frame score likes all four. The possession score says the raw version is worthless: a respectable 0.65 per frame, but 0.05 per possession, because it flips constantly and invents hundreds of possessions that never happened.

To a trader this is hit rate against profit per trade. A strategy that flips position every bar can have a fine hit rate and still be a disaster, because it trades 500 times when it should trade 50. spellF1 counts trades. Every false flip is a fake possession, and a fake possession costs as much as a missed one.

spellF1 also hates being late:

Figure 2. A perfect answer, shifted later by a few frames, scored with spellF1 (cases from the organisers' documentation; the line joins the five measured points). One frame late is free. A second late costs a third of the score.

That's latency. In a fast market, a correct signal that arrives a second late is a different and worse signal. Here it's the same: a change of possession placed a second after it happened is treated almost like a wrong answer.

The quant reflex

Before writing any code I wrote down, in one sentence, what the metric rewards: few, clean, well-timed changes. That sentence decided the design. A model that gives the probability of each state on every frame, and a separate rule that charges for every change and is allowed to look at the whole half before placing one. Both are below.

§ 5Seventeen Trades Is Not a Backtest

The organisers publish the answers for the first 5 minutes. It's tempting. It's also 17 possessions. On 17 possessions, one possession right or wrong moves spellF1 by about 0.06. The 90% bootstrap interval on our own public score runs from 0.45 to 0.83.

You wouldn't size a strategy off 17 trades. You certainly wouldn't pick the best of ten strategies with them, because you'd just pick the luckiest one. I wrote about exactly this trap in Breeding the Perfect Candlestick Pattern: search hard enough on a small sample and you will always find something beautiful that isn't real. The rules agree. The 5 minutes are a pipeline check, not a tuning set.

So I fixed a validation protocol before building any model, borrowed straight from strategy research:

  1. Leave one match out, over 10 labelled public matches from other leagues. Train on nine, score the tenth, rotate. Walk-forward testing, but across markets instead of across time.
  2. Nested: redo every choice (blend weights, decoder costs) using only the nine training matches, then score the tenth. The gap between this and step 1 is the price of choosing. It's the same idea as deflating a Sharpe ratio for the number of strategies you tried.
  3. An untouched set: 20 matches from another league and another tracking provider, scored once at the end. A different exchange.
  4. The real match's public 5 minutes, scored once, after everything was frozen. Day one of paper trading.

This protocol killed my first idea. It was a hand-built model: the nearest player to the ball, how fast players move, whether the ball sits on a corner spot, a referee-whistle detector on the audio, shirt colours near the ball from the video. Lots of weights, all set by hand. It looked clever. It's also exactly the kind of model that only looks clever on the data its weights were set on. Fitted honestly on other matches and scored on held-out ones, it managed about 0.56. The learned model in the rest of this post scores 0.715.

The lesson I keep relearning

If you can't fit a weight out of sample, it isn't a parameter. It's an opinion. A model full of opinions can look great on the five minutes you looked at, and it tells you nothing about the hundred you didn't.

§ 6Borrowing Other Markets

You don't backtest a Nifty strategy on one month of Nifty. You use every market and every year you can find. Same here: the model learns from other matches that come with labels, and nothing was labelled by us.

Why these three? DFL's ball-status flag is the closest public match to the challenge's dead ball. Metrica's tracking comes from the same kind of automatic tracker as our match, with its own real ball gaps. SkillCorner is broadcast tracking from yet another provider, which makes it a stress test.

One translation was needed. DFL's possession flag follows every touch, but the challenge wants control. So I wrote one rule: a team spell shorter than 2 seconds that ends with the previous team back on the ball, or with play stopping, is a touch, not a possession, and goes back to the previous team. Two seconds is the shortest reading of the challenge's own hint, "a player keeps the ball for a few seconds". I fixed it before running a single experiment and never tuned it.

That's the same discipline as defining what counts as a trade in a messy fill log before you look at the P&L. Fix the rule first, so you can't bend it later.

§ 7Make the Backtest Feel Like Live

Every quant learns this the hard way. A backtest on clean prices with no costs, no slippage and no missing quotes is a fantasy. Live trading has all three. The fix is to put the frictions into the backtest.

DFL's tracking is clean: the ball is always there and all 22 players are always tracked. Ours is not. A model trained on clean data learns to lean on a ball that, in our match, is missing 41% of the time. So every DFL match is degraded with statistics measured on our match's tracking alone, no labels involved:

  • Ball blackouts. Each DFL half gets a continuous stretch of our match's own ball-visibility pattern, pasted on from a random starting point.
  • Missing players. Players are dropped to match our tracker's count of visible players per team, frame by frame, re-choosing who disappears every 10 seconds.
  • Noise. A slowly drifting 0.3 m error per player and 0.2 m on the ball, set in advance from typical optical-tracking error.
  • Metrica already has real gaps from the same kind of tracker, so it only gets the missing players.

Then I did it twice. Every match is degraded into two copies with different random blackouts and drop-outs. Same reason you run a backtest under many Monte Carlo slippage paths instead of one: the model shouldn't get comfortable with one particular pattern of bad luck.

The subtle part, and the one I'm proudest of

In our match the ball usually goes missing when play stops. So a missing ball is strong evidence of a dead ball, and the obvious move is to bake that link into the training data. I deliberately didn't. The blackouts are pasted on at random, independent of the labels, so in training a missing ball tells the model nothing about the answer. How strongly a blackout signals a stoppage depends on this tracker and this camera operator. Learn it from Bundesliga data and you learn the Bundesliga's tracker. In trading terms: don't let your strategy learn your data vendor's quirks. Model the vendor separately, on the data you'll actually trade. That's § 11.

§ 8Two Feeds, One Clock

The tactical camera loses the ball 41% of the time. But there's a second feed: the TV broadcast, with its own automatic tracking and its own camera-to-pitch map. It runs on its own clock, with cuts and replays, and it isn't synchronised. To a quant that's a familiar picture: two exchanges quoting the same asset, one of them late. Find the lag, then use one feed to fill holes in the other.

Within a half the broadcast runs at a constant delay, so the lag is whatever lines the two ball tracks up best:

\hat{\ell} = \arg\min_{\ell}\ \operatorname{median}_{t}\ \left\lVert\, b^{\text{tactical}}_{t} - b^{\text{broadcast}}_{t+\ell} \,\right\rVert
(2)

I searched coarse to fine: one-second averages over a wide range of lags, then frame by frame. The answer is 4,014 frames (about 2 min 41 s) in the first half and 26,920 frames (about 18 minutes) in the second. At that lag the two balls sit a median 0.8 m apart. No video pixels and no labels, just two ball tracks lined up like two price series.

Where the tactical ball is missing, I take the broadcast's ball, but only when its pitch map is trustworthy (reprojection error under 5 m) and the ball doesn't jump more than 3 m. That fills 8,049 frames and lifts ball visibility from 59.3% to 64.4%.

The broadcast gives one more thing for free. When it has no pitch map at the aligned frame, the director is showing a replay, a close-up or the crowd, and directors do that mostly when the ball is dead. Think of a business channel cutting to the studio: you can guess the market's quiet without seeing a single price. That becomes the cut-away flag.

§ 9The Signal: 219 Numbers per Frame

None of the features is about a specific player. Tracks swap identity constantly, so anything player-specific would be noise. Everything describes a team or the ball, each team seen in its own attacking direction:

  • Who's near the ball: each team's nearest and second-nearest player, and how many players are within 2, 5 and 10 m.
  • Team shape: centre, spread, the most advanced and the deepest player.
  • Movement: average speed, the share of players running and walking, speed towards goal. When play stops, players walk.
  • Ball state: ball speed, ball still, ball beyond a line, ball on a restart spot (corner, centre, touchline), time since and until the ball was last and next seen, length of the current gap.
  • Context: 24 key features also averaged over 1, 3 and 9 second windows, and over the past and the future 2 and 6 seconds.

Yes, the future. In trading that's look-ahead bias and a firing offence. Here it isn't: the job is to label a finished match, not to trade it live, and a stoppage is far easier to recognise when you can see the restart coming. Knowing which of the two worlds you're in is half of avoiding the bias.

Every team feature comes as a home/away pair, so swapping the teams is just a reshuffle of columns. The model trains on both views, and at prediction time it runs on the match and on its mirror image, with \tilde{x} the match with the teams swapped:

p_H(x) = \tfrac{1}{2}\left[f_H(x) + f_A(\tilde{x})\right], \qquad p_A(x) = \tfrac{1}{2}\left[f_A(x) + f_H(\tilde{x})\right]
(3)

So the model can't favour a side. This is a market-neutral idea: if you flip every input, the signal must flip too. If it doesn't, you've learned a bias, not an edge.

§ 10A Small Portfolio of Models

Three kinds of model, each with a different job.

LightGBM is gradient-boosted trees: a few hundred small decision trees, each one correcting the last one's mistakes. It's fast, it's strong on tables of features like these, and in deterministic mode it retrains to the same file byte for byte. It trains on both degraded copies of every match, at 5 frames a second.

A bidirectional GRU is a small recurrent neural network that reads the match like a sentence, forwards and backwards, over 51-second windows. It's good exactly where the trees struggle: long stretches where the ball isn't seen and the answer has to come from context. I train three of them with different random seeds and average them. Training uses PyTorch; for the submission I re-implemented the network in plain numpy (matching PyTorch to within 4\times10^{-7}), so making the file needs no deep-learning library at all.

p = 0.6\,p_{\text{LightGBM}} + 0.4 \cdot \tfrac{1}{3}\sum_{i=1}^{3} p_{\text{GRU},\,i}
(4)

A boundary model, a second LightGBM, asks a narrower question: is a direct change of team happening within half a second of this frame? On held-out matches it averages 0.19 on real turnover frames and 0.02 everywhere else. It doesn't label anything. It sets prices, in § 12.

An ensemble is a portfolio. Two models that make different mistakes, blended, beat either one alone, for the same reason two imperfectly correlated assets beat one. And averaging seeds is parameter robustness: I don't trust a strategy that only works with one lookback, and I don't trust a network that only works with one random seed.

  • All 10 held-out matches land between 0.66 and 0.73 (Figure 6 below).
  • The untouched SkillCorner set moved the same way, 0.555 to 0.566, though it played no part in any choice.
  • Every change that didn't survive these checks was dropped (§ 14).

§ 11Evidence Without Labels

Back to the missing ball. I kept it out of the training labels on purpose. Now I want it back, measured on our match, without any answers. The tool is EM, short for Expectation–Maximisation, and the one-step version I use is simple:

  1. The model already gives every frame a probability that the ball is dead.
  2. Put each frame in a bucket: ball seen, or the length of the blackout it sits in. Separately: broadcast showing the pitch, or cut away.
  3. For each bucket, add up how much dead-ball probability lands in it, and how much live-ball probability. The log of the ratio of their shares is that bucket's evidence.
e_k = \tfrac{1}{2}\left[\,\ln\frac{\sum_{t:\,k_t=k} p^{D}_{t}}{\sum_{t} p^{D}_{t}} \;-\; \ln\frac{\sum_{t:\,k_t=k} \left(1-p^{D}_{t}\right)}{\sum_{t} \left(1-p^{D}_{t}\right)}\,\right]
(5)

p^D_t is the model's dead-ball probability on frame t, and k_t is the frame's bucket. The evidence is added to the dead-ball score in the decoder.

The \tfrac{1}{2} in front is shrinkage, fixed in advance. An estimate built from the model's own beliefs deserves a bit of humility. Here is what comes out on our match:

Figure 5. Log-odds evidence for a stoppage, estimated on the match without any labels (before the half weight). Red pushes towards dead ball, grey towards live play.

It's the pattern you'd hope for. The longer the blackout, the stronger the case for a stoppage, rising steadily from +0.62 to +2.52. A cut-away pushes towards dead, a live picture towards play. Nothing here saw an answer.

Before adopting the blackout evidence I tested it on the held-out external matches. On Metrica, whose gaps are real, it added +0.009. On DFL, whose gaps I pasted on at random, it did nothing, which is exactly what should happen. That's a placebo test, and passing it matters as much as the gain. The cut-away flag couldn't be tested this way, since no external match comes with a broadcast feed. It's in because it's label-free and it points the expected way.

Where the idea came from

Regime models. A hidden Markov model separates calm and stormy markets without anyone labelling the regimes, by asking which state best explains what you see. Same move here, with "live" and "dead" as the regimes and blackouts as the thing you see.

§ 12Charge for Every Switch

Now the most quant part of the whole thing. The models give a probability for H, A and dead on every frame. Take the most likely label frame by frame and you get thousands of flips, the "trade every bar" disaster from § 3. You need a rule that only changes position when the evidence is strong and sustained. Traders already know that rule. It's a transaction cost.

The decoder is Viterbi. It finds the single best path of labels through a whole half, where every frame earns the log-probability of its label and every change pays a price:

\hat{s}_{1:T} = \arg\max_{s_{1:T}}\ \sum_{t=1}^{T}\Big[\ln p_t(s_t) + e_t\,\mathbb{1}[s_t = D]\Big] \;-\; \sum_{t=2}^{T} c(s_{t-1}, s_t)
(6)

A switch from Barcelona to Athletic costs 12, in the same log units. Stopping or restarting play also costs 12. A brief burst of evidence for the other team can't pay that, so a deflection or a 50/50 doesn't flip possession. A real turnover produces sustained evidence that easily does. That is the challenge's "controlled possession" rule, written as a price.

Here is the trade-off on a simulated five minutes: made-up data with a known truth, not the match. The raw guess is right on most frames and still invents dozens of possessions. Slide the cost up.

At zero cost the decoder is just the raw guess: 164 possessions against 25 real ones, spellF1 0.14, even though 82% of frames are right. Around a cost of 7 it peaks at 0.86. Push it to 40 and it under-trades: real changes get merged away and spellF1 falls back to 0.38, while the share of frames right ends where it started. Too cheap and you overtrade, too expensive and you never trade. Frame accuracy hardly notices either mistake.

One fixed price is crude, though. A real turnover is a moment when switching should be cheap. So in the submission the switch cost moves with the boundary model's probability b_t:

c_{\text{team}}(t) = 12\,\left(1 - 0.9\,b_t\right), \qquad c_{\text{dead}} = 12
(7)

At a near-certain turnover the price drops to 1.2. Everywhere else it stays close to 12. It's the same as trading cheaply when the order book is deep and expensively when it's thin. This step added +0.004 out of sample, and it made short possessions split where they really split instead of being swallowed by their neighbours.

One more thing, and it's the reverse of everything trading teaches. In a market you can't see tomorrow, so every filter lags. Here the whole half is known before a single label is written, so Viterbi works as a smoother, not a filter: it places each change where the evidence changes, not where a causal rule would finally notice. With spellF1 charging a third of the score for a one-second delay, that's worth a lot.

Where the idea came from

I'd already written a whole post on how costs eat strategies, Transaction Costs Are a Black Hole. This decoder is the flip side of that post. If every change costs you, you only change when it pays. The cost and the blend weight were chosen together on the held-out matches, never on the real one.

§ 15Reproducible or It Didn't Happen

The rules demand that the public repository regenerates the submitted file exactly. Not approximately, exactly. I treated that the way I treat a backtest: if I can't rerun it and get the same equity curve, I don't have a result.

  • One command, under a minute on a laptop CPU. It reads only tracking and pitch maps, no video.
  • The output has SHA-256 9b029648… on Windows (Python 3.11) and Linux (Python 3.12), starting from empty caches.
  • LightGBM retrains to identical model files. The GRUs train with fixed seeds and deterministic kernels.
  • Every external dataset is pinned to an exact version, and no match data sits in the repository.

The code is public: submission_MTAH2027_EM-FC.

§ 16Limits, and What I'd Do Next

  • The labels are proxies. The 2-second control rule is a reasoned default, not a measured one, and Metrica's possession comes from event data.
  • Short possessions still get merged. On the public window we predicted 13 possessions against 17.
  • Stoppages with the ball in view, like a quick free kick or an injury near the ball, can be caught late, because the model leans on players slowing down.
  • Some sizes can't be checked without the answer key: the degrader's noise levels and the cut-away weight.

With more time:

  • More labelled matches, especially ones where the ball stays tracked through stoppages.
  • A model of each team's controlling player, not just team averages.
  • A duration model for possessions (a semi-Markov decoder). In trading terms, a model of holding periods, not just of signals.

§ 17What to Carry Forward

  1. Read the scoring rule before building anything. It is the strategy.
  2. A tiny test set is a trap. Look at it once, at the end.
  3. Train on other markets, but make them look like yours. The frictions belong in the backtest.
  4. Keep data quirks out of the labels, and estimate them separately, without answers.
  5. Every switch has a cost. Charge it, and make it cheap only where a change is likely.
  6. Small gains are noise until several independent checks agree.
  7. If you can't reproduce it byte for byte, you don't have a result.

Whatever the leaderboard says on 26 November, the thing I'll keep is this. A football match and a market are both noisy streams hiding a state you can't see, and the tools carry over almost untouched. That's what made it more than a hack.
