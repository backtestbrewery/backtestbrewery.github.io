The Professional Trader

2026-09-22 IIT Roorkee · Trading Costs · Break-even · SEBI 6 min read

Shoutout

This post is a remix of The Professional Trader, written in 2013 by NBBO2OBBN on the hacking NASDAQ @ 500 FPS blog. Same question, same kind of arithmetic, new country and a new decade.

A professional trader is someone who pays their bills from the market. A good month doesn't count. The rent, the groceries and the EMI have to come out of it every month, including the bad ones.

That sounds like a fair definition until you try to price it. How many trades does it take to replace a salary? The 2013 post ran that sum for a US computer science graduate. I wanted the Indian version, with a benchmark closer to home: the average IIT Roorkee placement. Same division, different numbers.

Short version. The salary turns out to be a tiny amount per share and a huge number of trades, and the charges eat most of what those trades earn.

§ 1 The Benchmark

Start with the salary. The latest reported average CTC for IIT Roorkee B.Tech graduates, from the 2024-25 season, is ₹24.96 LPA. I'll round it to ₹25 LPA. Divide by the number of trading days in a year, about 250, and you get what one day of that salary is worth:

T = CTC / D = 25,00,000 / 250 = ₹10,000(1)

T is the profit you need to keep on each trading day and D is the number of trading days. It also works out to about ₹2.08 lakh a month and ₹50,000 a week.

Two caveats. CTC is not what lands in your account, and I have not touched tax anywhere in this post. And an average gets dragged up by a handful of very large offers. The NIRF 2026 median for the 4-year UG batch is ₹18 LPA, so treat ₹25 LPA as the generous benchmark. Every trade count below scales in proportion to the target, so you can swap in your own number.

§ 2 One Day, Spread Over Shares

If you trade q shares in a day, the net profit you need on each one is

p = T / q(2)

I'll stay on the 10,000 share row. A rupee a share sounds very doable on paper. Then the charges show up.

§ 3 The Charges

Say the stock costs ₹500. On the NSE a stock in that price range moves in steps of 5 paise. You buy 200 shares (₹1 lakh) and sell them the same day. I ran that trade through Zerodha's brokerage calculator to get the actual charges rather than guess at them. It's a good free tool if you want to try your own price and quantity. Here is the round trip:

c = 82.68 / 200 = ₹0.413 per share(3)

One tick on this stock is 5 paise, so c is a little over eight ticks.

Charges shift a little between brokers, but the size is what matters. Eight ticks are gone before you earn a rupee.

§ 4 A Realistic Hit Rate

The 2013 post gave its trader a 60% hit rate, so I'll do the same. Sixty percent of trades win, 40% lose, and each one moves w rupees a share either way. Take w = ₹3, which is a 0.6% move on a ₹500 stock. Averaging over winners and losers, the expected gross profit per share is

E[g] = h·w − (1 − h)·w = (2h − 1)·w(4)
        = 0.2 × ₹3 = ₹0.60

g is the gross profit per share and h is the hit rate. A 60% hit rate with equal sized wins and losses leaves you a fifth of one move.

Now take out the charges:

n = E[g] − c = 0.60 − 0.413 = ₹0.187 per share(5)

You keep about 19 paise a share. To keep ₹10,000 a day at that rate, you need far more than 10,000 shares:

q* = T / n = 10,000 / 0.187 ≈ 53,476 shares(6)
R = q* / 200 ≈ 267 round trips

That is 53,476 shares, not 10,000. In trades of 200 shares it comes to 267 round trips a day.

§ 5 How Often You Would Have to Trade

The cash market runs 9:15 to 3:30, which is 6 hours 15 minutes, or 22,500 seconds. Spread 267 round trips across that and you get a round trip every 84 seconds. Each round trip is an entry and an exit, so the gap that matters is between orders:

t = S / (2R) = 22,500 / (2 × 267) ≈ 42 seconds(7)

S is the length of the session in seconds.

An order about every 42 seconds, all session, at a 60% hit rate, for 250 days. Here is one day on a single sheet.

Charges take 69% of what you earn. The job pays that ₹10,000 for a normal day of work. The market wants 267 round trips for it, and I haven't even touched tax.

The ₹3 move is doing a lot of the work here. Shrink it and the picture turns ugly quickly. Net per share is (2h − 1)w − c, which hits zero at a break-even move w*, and the trades you need blow up as w gets close to it:

w* = c / (2h − 1) = 0.413 / 0.2 ≈ ₹2.07(8)
R(w) = T / (200 · ((2h − 1)w − c)) = 250 / (w − w*)(9)

200 is the number of shares per trade. Below w*, the edge does not cover the charges and no amount of volume fixes it.

Worth a pause

A 60% hit rate sounds like a real edge. On a ₹500 stock with these charges it only pays once each win and loss is bigger than ₹2.07, which is about 0.41% of the price. Below that line you are paying for the privilege of being right most of the time.

§ 6 What SEBI Found

That was cash stocks and a tidy hit rate. Derivatives are rougher, and SEBI has counted. Its FY26 study of individual traders in equity F&O found:

MeasureFY26
Individual F&O traders who lost money87.7%
Traders under 30 who lost money88.5%
Net loss of individual traders, in total₹91,685 crore
Average loss per trader₹1.17 lakh

The average loss is about twelve days of the ₹10,000 target we started with. On top of that, since 1 April 2026 STT on futures has gone from 0.02% to 0.05%, and STT on option premium from 0.10% to 0.15%. That pushes the break-even move up for anyone scalping derivatives. The game got pricier.
