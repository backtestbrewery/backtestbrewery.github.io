What Volatility Actually Is

2026-11-18 Nifty 50 · Volatility · Fat Tails · Volatility Drag 10 min read

Everyone agrees the Nifty is volatile. Ask how volatile and you get a feeling, not a number.

This is a four part series that turns the feeling into a number, then takes apart everything that number hides. No options yet, just prices. By the end of Part 4 we'll rebuild something close to India VIX using nothing but daily candles.

Part 1 is the foundation: what volatility is, what it quietly assumes, and where it lies to you. Every number below comes from 5,126 Nifty 50 sessions, 2 January 2006 to 13 October 2026.

§ 1Points Lie, Percentages Don't

In January 2006 the Nifty was at 2,836. In October 2026 it closed at 22,722. A 300 point fall was a panic in 2006 and a shrug today. So step one is always the same: forget points, use returns.

I use log returns:

r_t = \ln\frac{C_t}{C_{t-1}}
(1)

Two reasons. Logs add up, so a month's return is just the sum of its daily returns. And they're honest about losses. Gain 10% then lose 10% and you're down 1%. In logs that's +0.0953 then -0.1054, which sums to -0.0101, the actual loss.

Figure 1. Top: the Nifty on a log scale. Bottom: every daily return since 2006, red where the move was bigger than four standard deviations.

The price chart looks like a steady climb. The returns chart is nothing but drama, and the drama arrives in bunches. Hold on to that second point. It's the most important thing in this post.

§ 2One Number for All of That

Volatility is the standard deviation of returns:

\hat{\sigma} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left(r_t - \bar{r}\right)^2}
(2)

For the Nifty, the daily \hat{\sigma} is 1.32%. The average daily return is 0.04%. The noise is 32 times the drift. On any single day the drift is a rounding error and the wobble is the whole story.

That one formula makes four assumptions. Worth saying them out loud, because the rest of this series is about what happens when each one breaks:

  • Every day comes from the same distribution. A sleepy Tuesday in 2017 and the Covid crash are treated as samples of one thing.
  • Up and down count the same. A +3% day adds exactly as much volatility as a −3% day.
  • Big days dominate. Squaring makes them loud. The biggest 1% of days, 51 sessions, account for 27% of all the variance in 21 years. The biggest 5% account for 53%.
  • Order doesn't matter. Shuffle the 5,126 days into random order and \hat{\sigma} doesn't move. Your experience of holding the index would change completely.

§ 3Scaling With Time

Most volatility numbers you see are annual. Getting there from daily takes one assumption: days are independent. Then variances add, and standard deviations grow with the square root of time:

\sigma_T = \sigma_1\sqrt{T} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \sigma_{\text{annual}} = 1.32\% \times \sqrt{252} \approx 20.9\%
(3)

The Nifty actually averages about 247 sessions a year, which gives 20.7%. Everyone uses 252. The difference is noise.

The rule of 16

\sqrt{252} \approx 15.9, so divide an annual volatility by 16 to get a typical daily move. A market running at 16% volatility moves about 1% a day. This comes back in a big way in Part 4, when India VIX shows up.

Does the square root rule actually hold? Test it with the variance ratio: the variance of T-day returns divided by T times the daily variance. If days are independent, it sits at 1.

VR(T) = \frac{\operatorname{Var}\left(r_t + r_{t-1} + \cdots + r_{t-T+1}\right)}{T\,\operatorname{Var}(r_t)}
(4)

Figure 2. Variance ratio of Nifty returns for horizons of 1 to 126 trading days, using overlapping windows. The shaded band is two standard errors either side of 1, where pure noise would land.

It stays between 1.00 and 1.10 at every horizon, and apart from a whisker at two days it stays inside the band. The rule passes. Daily returns barely remember yesterday: the correlation between today's return and yesterday's is 0.03.

Here's the twist. Returns don't remember their direction. They do remember their size.

hover for values

Figure 3. Correlation of today's return with the return k days earlier (grey), and the same for absolute returns (red). The shaded strip is where pure noise would land.

Today's return tells you almost nothing about tomorrow's direction. It tells you a lot about tomorrow's size. The correlation of |r_t| with yesterday's |r_{t-1}| is 0.29, and it's still 0.15 three months later. Big days follow big days, quiet days follow quiet days.

This is volatility clustering, and it's exactly what the fourth assumption in § 2 throws away. Shuffling the days keeps \hat{\sigma} and destroys this pattern. Part 3 is built entirely on it.

§ 4Volatility Is Not Risk

If Nifty returns were normal with a 1.32% daily standard deviation, here's how often big days should show up, against how often they did:

A 4σ day should come about once in a lifetime. The Nifty had 42 of them in 21 years. 23 March 2020 was a 10.6σ day, and under a normal curve a 10σ day simply doesn't happen.

Figure 4. Daily returns in standard deviation units, against the normal curve. Top: the usual view, where the tails are invisible slivers. Bottom: the same counts on a log scale, red beyond 3σ. Two days don't fit on the chart at all.

The shape gives it away: too many quiet days near zero, too few medium days, far too many monsters. Statisticians call this kurtosis. A normal distribution has excess kurtosis 0. The Nifty's is 13.4. The skew is −0.34, so the extreme days lean slightly down.

So \sigma measures the typical wobble, not how bad it gets. Two markets with the same \sigma can have very different crashes.

Why are the tails so fat? Part of the answer is in this chart:

Figure 5. Annualized volatility of the Nifty for each calendar year. Red: the two crisis years. 2017, the calmest, is picked out too. 2026 runs to 13 October.

The same index ran at 45% volatility in 2008 and 9% in 2017. Five times apart. A single \sigma for both years is like quoting one average depth for a river in the monsoon and in May.

What surprised me

I assumed fat tails were a property of returns themselves. Then I divided each day's return by a running estimate of recent volatility instead of one global \sigma (an exponentially weighted one, which Part 2 explains). Excess kurtosis fell from 13.4 to 2.2. The 4σ days fell from 42 to 15. Most of the fat tail is the market switching between calm and storm. The rest is genuinely fat.

§ 5Volatility Costs You Money

Lose 50%, then gain 50%. Average return: zero. Your money: down 25%. Volatility doesn't just make the ride bumpy, it eats growth. The rule is

g \approx \mu - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2
(5)

\mu is the average simple return, g is the rate the typical investor actually compounds at.

The Nifty shows this almost to the decimal. Its average daily return was 0.0492%. Its average daily log return, the thing that actually compounds, was 0.0406%. The gap is 0.00865% a day. Half the daily variance is 0.00863%.

Over a year of 247 sessions that's an average return of 12.1%, a compounding rate of 10.0% in log terms (a 10.5% CAGR), and a gap of 2.1 points. All of it is drag.

Drag grows with the square of volatility. Double \sigma and you quadruple the drag, which is why 2x leveraged products bleed in choppy markets. Try it:

§ 6The Volatility You Never See

Here's the uncomfortable part. You never observe volatility. You see prices. Volatility is a property of the process that made them, it keeps changing, and everything you compute is an estimate of it. People use one word for four different things:

  • Realized (or historical): measured from past returns. Everything above.
  • Forecast: a model's guess for the next day, week or month. Part 3.
  • Implied: the market's guess, priced into options. India VIX is one. Part 4.
  • True: the thing all three are chasing. Nobody gets to see it.

To see the gap between true and realized, I simulated a market where I set the truth myself: calm for a while, then a shock that fades with a 30 day half-life. Then I measured it the standard way, with a rolling 21 day window.

hover for values

Figure 6. A simulated market. The plain line is the true volatility, which I chose. Red is what a 21 day close to close estimate sees.

On the day of the shock the truth is 45%. The estimate says 20%. It peaks 19 days later at 41%, when the truth has already cooled to 35%. Late on the way up, late on the way down, and off by about two points even when nothing is happening.

That's not a bad estimator. That's what estimating a moving target from 21 noisy numbers looks like.

§ 7What to Carry Forward

  1. Volatility is the standard deviation of returns. Use log returns.
  2. It scales with \sqrt{T}. Divide annual volatility by 16 for a typical day.
  3. Returns forget their direction but remember their size. That's clustering.
  4. \sigma is not tail risk. Most fat tails come from volatility changing over time.
  5. Volatility costs growth: g \approx \mu - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2.
  6. You never see true volatility. You only estimate it, and estimates lag.

Which raises the obvious question: how do you estimate it well? Every daily candle hands you an open, a high, a low, a close and a volume. The standard formula uses one of them. Part 2 uses all five.
