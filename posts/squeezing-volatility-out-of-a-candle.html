Squeezing Volatility Out of a Candle

2026-11-26 Nifty 50 · OHLCV · Range Estimators · Yang Zhang 11 min read

Every trading day the exchange hands you five numbers: open, high, low, close and volume. The volatility formula from Part 1 uses one of them.

Picture a day where the Nifty falls 3% by lunch and claws it all back by 3:30. Close to close says nothing happened. Anyone who held through it knows otherwise.

This part squeezes the whole candle: which estimators to use, how much better they are, where they break on real Nifty data, and what volume adds. The sample is 3,948 sessions, 18 October 2010 to 13 October 2026. Why 2010 is its own story, in § 5.

§ 1The Honest Baseline

Close to close first, because everything else gets checked against it:

\hat{\sigma}^2_{CC} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n} r_t^2, \qquad r_t = \ln\frac{C_t}{C_{t-1}}
(1)

I drop the mean. Part 1 showed it's about 3% of \sigma, so estimating it adds noise and buys nothing.

Now the catch. Even with perfectly normal returns, a volatility estimate from n days carries a relative error of about

\frac{\operatorname{SE}(\hat{\sigma})}{\sigma} \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{2n}}
(2)

Twenty-one days gives ±15%. A "16% volatility" reading from a month of data really means somewhere between 13.5% and 18.5%, and that's the optimistic version; fat tails make it worse. A full year gets you to ±4.5%, but by then the number is a year old.

That's the window trade-off in one picture. The 21 day line catches the Covid spike (88% on 9 April 2020) and the 2017 lull (5.4% in June). The 252 day line misses both, then stays high for a year after the panic ended.

§ 2The Ghost in the Window

Hard windows have a nasty side effect. A big day sits in the window at full weight for exactly n days, then vanishes all at once.

hover for values

Figure 2. February to June 2020. The 21 day window against an exponentially weighted estimate.

23 March 2020, a −13.9% day, entered the 21 day window and pinned it up. On 27 April, a calm +1.4% day, 21 day volatility fell from 71.7% to 53.4%. Nothing happened on 27 April. A month-old crash just aged out. That's the ghost.

The fix is to let old days fade instead of fall off. That's the exponentially weighted moving average, EWMA:

\hat{\sigma}^2_t = \lambda\,\hat{\sigma}^2_{t-1} + (1-\lambda)\,r_t^2, \qquad \lambda = 0.94
(3)

Yesterday's estimate, nudged toward today's squared return. Every past day still counts, with its weight shrinking 6% a day. \lambda = 0.94 is the old RiskMetrics choice and gives a half-life of about 11 days. On 27 April the EWMA moved from 58.9% to 57.4%. No ghost.

§ 3What the High and Low Know

Close to close sees one point a day. The high and low see the extremes of the whole path. If the price wanders randomly inside the day, the squared range is tied directly to the variance:

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\ln\frac{H}{L}\right)^2\right] = 4\ln 2\;\sigma^2
(4)

Flip that around and you have an estimator. The four classics below each fix the previous one's blind spot. Read them as a story, not a menu.

Parkinson (1980) uses only the range. It assumes no drift and no jump between yesterday's close and today's open.

\hat{\sigma}^2_{P} = \frac{1}{4\ln 2}\left(\ln\frac{H_t}{L_t}\right)^2
(5)

Garman and Klass (1980) add the open and close. Sharper, same assumptions.

\hat{\sigma}^2_{GK} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\ln\frac{H_t}{L_t}\right)^2 - \left(2\ln 2 - 1\right)\left(\ln\frac{C_t}{O_t}\right)^2
(6)

Rogers and Satchell (1991) rebuild it so a trending day doesn't inflate the number. Still blind to the overnight gap.

\hat{\sigma}^2_{RS} = \ln\frac{H_t}{C_t}\ln\frac{H_t}{O_t} + \ln\frac{L_t}{C_t}\ln\frac{L_t}{O_t}
(7)

Yang and Zhang (2000) glue the night back on. Over a window of n days, combine the variance of overnight returns \ln(O_t/C_{t-1}), the variance of open to close returns \ln(C_t/O_t), and Rogers Satchell:

\hat{\sigma}^2_{YZ} = \hat{\sigma}^2_{O} + k\,\hat{\sigma}^2_{C} + (1-k)\,\hat{\sigma}^2_{RS}, \qquad k = \frac{0.34}{1.34 + \frac{n+1}{n-1}}
(8)

It handles drift and gaps. The price is that it needs a window; there's no single-day version.

§ 4A Test Where I Know the Answer

On real data you never know the true volatility, so you can't tell which estimator is right. So I built a market where I do know. True volatility is fixed at 16%. Each day is 375 one-minute steps, the length of an NSE session. I simulated 4,000 months of 21 days and ran every estimator on each.

Then I did it twice: once with no overnight gap, and once with 35% of the variance arriving overnight, which is the Nifty's real share (§ 5).

Figure 3. Simulated 21 day estimates when the truth is 16%. Dots are averages; lines span the middle 90% of 4,000 estimates.

Three things jump out.

Ranges are far sharper. Close to close lands anywhere from 11.8% to 19.9%. Garman Klass stays inside 13.8% to 16.7%. In efficiency terms, one day of Garman Klass is worth about eight days of closes, and one day of Parkinson about five.

With a gap, three of them are confidently wrong. Parkinson, Garman Klass and Rogers Satchell only see the session. They miss 35% of the variance and settle around 12.3%. Tight and wrong is worse than loose and right, because it looks trustworthy.

Yang Zhang is the only one that's tight and right in both worlds.

One subtler thing. Even with no gap, the range estimators sit a little under 16%. With one-minute steps, the recorded high and low miss the true extremes between ticks. At 6,000 steps a day the bias vanishes and they all land at about 15.8%, level with close to close. (That last 0.2% is the square root pulling noisy estimates down a touch.) Real index data is sampled finely but not continuously, so expect range estimators to run slightly low in practice too.

§ 5The Nifty, by Day and by Night

First, a data trap. Before 18 October 2010, NSE's official Nifty open was often just the previous close, because the pre-open auction didn't exist yet. I dug into this in The Nifty Makes Its Money at Night. Any estimator that touches the open is garbage before that date, which is why this sample starts there.

A day's return is a night leg plus a day leg, r_t = o_t + c_t, so the variance splits too:

\operatorname{Var}(r_t) = \underbrace{\operatorname{Var}(o_t)}_{35.4\%} + \underbrace{\operatorname{Var}(c_t)}_{68.5\%} + \underbrace{2\operatorname{Cov}(o_t, c_t)}_{-3.9\%}
(9)

More than a third of the Nifty's daily variance happens while the market is shut. Annualized, the night runs at 9.8% volatility and the session at 13.6%. Now look at what that does to the estimators:

hover for values

Figure 4. Nifty volatility from a 63 day window, three ways: close to close, Parkinson (grey) and Yang Zhang (red).

Parkinson sits below close to close 96% of the time, at about 83% of its level. That gap is the missing night, exactly as the simulation predicted. Yang Zhang tracks close to close almost perfectly (median ratio 0.99) while moving about 20% less from one day to the next. Same level, less noise. That's the whole point of using the candle.

Now look at the flat-topped box in late 2012. On 5 October 2012 an erroneous order sent the Nifty's low to 4,888 for a few minutes, 15% under that day's close of 5,747. Close to close never noticed. Every range estimator did, and carried it at full weight for 63 days. Range estimators believe every print, including the wrong ones.

And the night is getting louder:

Figure 5. Share of the Nifty's daily variance that arrived overnight, by year. 2026 runs to 13 October.

Overnight carried 49% of the variance in 2025 and 61% so far in 2026. I don't know yet whether that's a regime or a run of noisy global nights. Either way, an estimator that ignores the gap is getting worse every year.

One more night fact. Monday's gap covers about 65 hours, nearly four times a weekday night. Its variance is only 1.9 times bigger. Variance follows news, not hours, and the world is quieter on weekends.

§ 6Where the V Comes In

Volume is the odd one out. It isn't a price, so no formula turns it into volatility. What it measures is how much information hit the market.

One caveat first: an index doesn't trade. The volume in NSE's Nifty file is the shares traded across its 50 members, and its level drifts with splits, bonus issues and index changes. It roughly tripled between 2017 and 2020. So raw volume is useless across years. I use relative volume: today's volume divided by the median of the previous 63 sessions.

Figure 6. Days sorted into ten equal buckets by relative volume. Bars show annualized Parkinson (session) volatility in each bucket.

The quietest tenth of days, at about 0.7 times normal volume, had session volatility of 8.6%. The busiest tenth, at 1.7 times, had 22.8%. This is the mixture of distributions idea from Clark (1973): returns look fat tailed partly because the amount of news varies from day to day, and volume is a gauge of news.

So does volume help predict tomorrow? I regressed tomorrow's log range variance on today's, the last week's and the last month's. That explains 31.1% of the variation. Adding today's relative volume takes it to 31.3%. Volume alone gets 3%. The range already knows almost everything volume knows.

Volume is a confirmation signal, not a volatility estimator.

§ 7Traps in Real Data

Checklist before trusting any number

Stale opens. Nifty opens before 18 October 2010 are often just the prior close.
Bad prints. One wrong high or low (5 October 2012) poisons every range estimator. Check the biggest ranges by eye.
Muhurat sessions. One hour, in the evening, tiny ranges. I drop them.
Special sessions. Weekend budget days and disaster recovery drills are real rows with odd ranges.
Weekends. Monday's overnight gap is bigger. Don't pool it blindly with other nights.
Annualizing. 252 is convention; the Nifty averages about 247 sessions a year.
Index volume. It's the members' volume, so only relative measures compare across years.
Sampling. A high or low is only as fine as the data that recorded it.

§ 8So Which One Do I Use?

  1. Close to close, always, as the baseline everything else gets checked against.
  2. Yang Zhang when the opens are clean and you care about total daily risk. For the Nifty after 2010, that's my default.
  3. Parkinson or Garman Klass only when you deliberately want session risk, say for an intraday strategy that's flat overnight.
  4. EWMA weights instead of hard windows, so old crashes fade instead of falling off a cliff.
  5. Relative volume as a confirmation, not an input.

Everything here measures volatility as it is now. But Part 1 showed that size remembers: today's volatility says a lot about tomorrow's. Part 3 turns that memory into forecasts with EWMA, GARCH and HAR, and takes on the harder question: how do you grade a forecast of a number nobody can see?
